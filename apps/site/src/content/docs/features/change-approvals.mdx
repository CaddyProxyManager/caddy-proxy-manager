---
title: Change approvals
description: Hold changes to hosts, access lists, the WAF and settings until another person approves them.
---

Some changes should be checked by another person first: turning off the WAF on the payment site, widening an
access list, a new global Caddy snippet. With **change approvals** on, such a change is not applied
when it is saved. It becomes a **change request**, and it applies only once enough approvers agree,
as the person who made it.

Requests and the policy live on the **Approvals** page.

## The policy

**Approvals → Approval policy** decides what waits. Anyone who can read settings sees it; only
someone who manages settings can change it.

| Setting | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Require approval | Off, every change applies when it is saved. Off by default |
| What needs approval | **Every change the policy can hold**, **changes in chosen areas** (hosts, access lists, the WAF, settings), or **changes to hosts with chosen tags** |
| Approver roles and groups | Who may approve: anyone holding one of the roles, their own or [through a group](../users-and-groups/), and anyone in one of the groups |
| Approvals each change needs | One or two |
| Apply to API tokens | On by default; see [API tokens](#api-tokens) |

A tag policy looks at the host's tags both before and after the change, so tagging a host into the
policy is held too, and so is removing the tag that put it there. Only host changes match a tag;
nothing else waits under a tag policy.

The policy is a settings change itself. While settings are covered, changing the policy is held for
approval like anything else.

### What the policy can hold

| Area | Changes |
| --- | --- |
| Hosts | Proxy and L4 hosts: creating, editing, enabling and disabling, maintenance mode, deleting, bulk changes, who may sign in to a host, its mTLS access rules, and [rolling back or restoring](../host-history/) a host |
| Access lists | Creating, editing and deleting a list, its IP rules, adding members and removing them |
| WAF | Presets, rule exclusions, and installing, updating, configuring or uninstalling CRS plugins |
| Settings | Applying staged settings (everything on the Settings pages that goes through **Review**), a settings group saved over the API, and the approval policy itself |

These writes are never held:

- **Regenerating an access list member's password** replaces a credential for someone who already
  has access; it changes nobody's access, and the new password is shown once, to whoever made it.
- **Settings that save at once** rather than through **Review** - the Caddy build, analytics,
  GeoIP, email, CAPTCHA and certificate alerts - and which CRS plugin registries are read.
- **Restoring a backup** and **importing a configuration** replace configuration wholesale. Only an
  administrator can do either, and an administrator can bypass approval anyway.
- **[Access reviews](../access-reviews/)** have reviewers of their own, and change users, groups and
  tokens, which no approval area covers.
- **Users, groups, roles, certificates, alerts and backups** are outside the four areas.

## Submitting a change

You edit as usual. When the policy covers what you are saving, the host editor's
review says so and its button reads **Submit** instead of **Save**; the staged settings review does
the same in place of **Apply**. Other pages save as always and tell you the change was submitted,
with its request number.

A submitted change runs every check a save would first, so a change that could never apply is
refused straight away rather than queued.

## Approving

Approvers see a banner on every page while a request waits for them. A request shows the same diff a
save would: for a host, the editor's own review with its impact - which agents reload, which
certificates are requested, and warnings such as a protection being removed; for staged settings,
the changed fields and the Caddy configuration they produce; for anything else, field by field.
Secrets are masked throughout.

- **Approve** adds your approval, with an optional note. The last approval a request needs applies
  it, **as the person who submitted it**: the host history and the audit log name them, with the
  request and its approvers recorded beside the change.
- **Reject** closes it. One rejection is enough.
- **Withdraw** is the requester's: it closes a request nobody has applied.

**Nobody approves their own change**, whatever their role, and a request needing two approvals needs
two people other than its requester.

### When the target changes first

A request remembers what it edits as it stood when submitted. If that changes before the request is
approved - someone edited the same host, or another request applied first - approving it does not
overwrite the newer state. The request goes **Out of date** and nothing is applied; make the change
again on top of what is there now.

Approval also never lends permission: if the requester has since lost the right to make the change,
or their account is disabled, the request fails rather than applying.

Two approvers pressing **Approve** at the same moment apply the change once.

If the controller stops while a request is applying, the request is settled after ten minutes in
that state, never by applying it again. A change that was written is marked applied; one that was
not fails, and the request says whether its target is untouched or that it cannot tell. In that
case, check the target before you submit the change again.

## Emergency bypass

When an outage cannot wait for an approver, an administrator can **Bypass** a pending request,
with a reason. It applies at once, still as its requester and still refused if its
target changed. Every bypass is in the audit log with the reason, and raises the **Change approval
bypassed** alert, which goes to the administrators' email and push by default and to any
[alert channel](../alerts/) a rule sends it to.

## API tokens

**Apply to API tokens** decides what a covered write made with an [API token](../api/) does.

- **On** (the default): it waits like any other. REST answers `202 Accepted` with
  `{ "status": "pending", "changeRequestId": 12 }`; GraphQL answers `202` with an error whose
  `extensions` carry `code: "PENDING_APPROVAL"` and the `changeRequestId`. The request is the token
  owner's. Read its outcome with the `changeRequest(id)` query.
- **Off**: the write applies at once, and its audit event records that approval was skipped.

Without the setting a token would be a way around the policy, which is why it is on by default.

Over GraphQL, `changeRequests`, `changeRequest`, `approveChangeRequest`, `rejectChangeRequest`,
`withdrawChangeRequest`, `bypassChangeRequest`, `approvalPolicy` and `setApprovalPolicy` do what
the page does. Deciding a request applies a change in whichever area it is in, so a token needs the
full scope to approve, reject or bypass one.

## Audit

Every step is in the [audit log](../audit-log/): submitted, approved, rejected, withdrawn, out of
date, bypassed with its reason, applied, or failed with why. The change itself is audited as the
requester's, with the request number and approvers in the event's details, and a host revision made
by a request links back to it from the host's history.
